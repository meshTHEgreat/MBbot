Insights — what this means and why

1. Seven generations of "no" just became one "why"

Every prior verdict said a particular signal failed. This one says something categorically different: across 148,441 observations, the market's implied intraday move exceeds its realized intraday move by $1.4–1.7 per straddle — before anyone pays a spread. The instrument is priced above what it delivers in our window. No trigger, filter, forecast, or execution tactic could have changed that, because the deficit exists before a strategy is even applied. Six generations weren't failing to find the signal; they were paying to enter a game with a negative table stake.

2. This is a measured negative, not an underpowered null — and that distinction is everything

The program's own critique was that its machinery could say "no" far more easily than "yes," and that low-power fails should be recorded as inconclusive. So the standard for this verdict was set higher: power at the frozen minimum effect reached 1.00 in the replication archive and 0.94–0.9998 in the admissible 2025–26 cells. The p-values are 1.000 against a positive gap — the maximum the test can express. If the buy side had a positive-EV state anywhere in the frozen grid, this design would have found it.

3. The mechanism came out backwards from the original thesis — and that's the interesting part

The first consultation attributed the buyer's disadvantage to the variance risk premium. The second corrected it: the literature localizes that premium to the overnight window, so an intraday-only mandate should largely sidestep it. Our archive says both are right and neither is sufficient. Intraday delta-hedged returns are indeed far less negative than to-expiry ones — the overnight dodge is real and measurable in our own data. Yet the intraday gap is still negative, so the retirement rationale lands somewhere sharper than either prediction: even after avoiding the overnight premium, the intraday implied move is priced above the realized one, and friction is charged on top of an already-negative expectation.

4. What the frozen escape hatch proves about the process

The decision rule was written, hashed, and registered before a single gap was computed, with a genuine way out: one preregistered state showing realized > implied + cost, replicated across archives, and the buy side stayed open. The rule then ran on data nobody had seen, produced an empty survivor list, and the verdict wrote itself. There was no judgment call to make — which is precisely why this answer can be trusted in a way that none of the program's earlier "we decided to stop" moments could be.

5. What survives, and where the machinery points now

The engine, the audits, the registry, the sealed blocks — all intact and now attached to a structural fact rather than a search. Three assets carry forward: the execution-technology deliverable (the replicated eligibility filter and the measured fill-bounds frontier — real, literature-backed, useful to any strategy on either side of the trade); the ship-as-shares design (the same directional questions, expressed in an instrument with no premium to overpay); and the short-premium scoping, which follows directly from this verdict — if buyers systematically overpay for the intraday move, the compensated side of that same trade is where the machinery should look next, under a mandate the user has yet to grant and a tail-risk budget it has yet to design.

Bottom line: the question "can we find a signal good enough to trade 1–4 DTE options intraday?" is answered — not by exhaustion, but by measurement. The instrument was never the right vehicle for the signals available. Knowing that, with receipts, after seven generations of increasingly disciplined search, is the most valuable thing this program has produced.
