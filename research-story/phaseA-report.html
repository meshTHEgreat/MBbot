    Insights — what this means and why

    1. The big reversal: friction was never the wall

    After Campaign 3, the natural story was "the signal is real but costs eat it — fix execution." Phase A tested that story properly and it fell. A perfect forecaster paying full costs wins in 95.2% of the option grid, earning $31–90 per trade in the very cells our strategy trades. Costs only look like a wall when the forecast is weak: $3.34 of edge against a $5.27 toll fails; $30 of edge wouldn't notice it. The constraint is forecast quality. This redirects the entire program: the next dollar goes to better prediction inputs, not to shaving pennies off execution — though execution calibration stays worthwhile precisely because weak-forecast eras are where every penny counts.

    2. The matched-baseline result is the humbling one

    L10 beats 93% of random entry sets taken under its own conditions — same mornings, same tight-spread moments, same contracts. Read that carefully: it means a meaningful share of the +$3.34 comes from the conditions (trading liquid contracts in the morning when spreads are tight) rather than from the breakout trigger itself. 93rd percentile is genuinely above average — but the program's own standard is 95%, and "would probably beat random" is not a deployable thesis. The honest statement: the conditions are validated; the trigger on top of them is unproven.

    3. The attribution is the encouraging one

    The edge decomposes into +$3.67 direction (Delta), +$1.36 convexity (Gamma), −$0.71 time decay (Theta), −$0.59 volatility (Vega), −$0.39 residual. That's the healthiest possible shape: money made by being right about direction, not by accidental volatility exposure or unexplained residue. Whatever forecast skill exists here is the kind that better inputs could amplify.

    4. The SPY/QQQ story dissolves — as predicted

    Campaign 3's tempting narrative ("it works on SPY!") is now measured: the +$5.20 per-trade gap between symbols sits at the 87th percentile of gaps produced by matched random sets — comfortably inside noise. Anyone who had rushed to trade "SPY only" would have been fitting a coin flip. This is why the concentration gate refused to pass it, and why post-hoc symbol selection stays banned.

    5. What the audits bought

    Six causal audits — truncation, within-bar exit ordering (zero ambiguous trades in 566), cost monotonicity, hand-reconstruction of 10 trades from raw quotes, session-close semantics, open-interest timing — all passed, and every Phase-A computation reruns byte-identically. The point is not celebration; it's that every number above survives the question "but is the backtest itself lying?" with receipts.

    6. The decision this sets up

    Two cheap purchases are justified now on existing entitlement: targeted tick windows (to calibrate what real fills could recover — the $5.27 conversion penalty is measured, not modeled) and Vega backfill (removes the one approximation in the attribution). The expensive decision — a true underlying stock-data feed to attack forecast quality directly — waits for the tick answer: if calibrated execution moves this trade class from −$1.93 toward breakeven, the current thread stays alive and earns the investment; if not, forecast-side data is the only path and the decision is made on evidence. Either way, the sealed validation and holdout blocks remain untouched — still the program's most valuable asset.

    Bottom line: feasibility proven almost everywhere, forecast skill proven almost nowhere. The program now knows exactly which problem it has — which, after three campaigns of learning which problems it doesn't have, is the most useful state it has ever been in.
