    Insights — what this means and why

    1. The signal is real-ish, and that is new

    Three campaigns, one arc. Campaign 1 (MACD on option premiums, 30 configurations): edge ≈ $0.00 before costs — the trigger predicted nothing; spread and commissions turned nothing into −$2,601. Campaign 2 (48 screened conditions): one family advanced, 51 trades, edge ≈ $0 again. Campaign 3: +$3.34 per trade before costs, across 566 trades, p ≈ 0.055. For the first time, a trigger fired on something other than noise.

    Why believe it more than the earlier false dawns? Sample size (566 vs 51), preregistration (the formula was frozen before the backtest), and the multiple-testing correction (56 candidates were screened; the q-value 0.055 already pays that penalty). Why not believe it fully? The 95% confidence interval [−$0.76, +$7.26] still touches zero — roughly a 1-in-18 chance a zero-edge trigger produces this by luck.

    2. Why it still failed: the toll booth

    Every round trip pays the spread twice (buy at ask, sell at bid) plus $1.30 commission. On these contracts that toll is $5–6 per trade. The signal earns $3.34. Earnings minus toll = −$1,090 across 566 trades. The gates that failed — profit factor 0.90 vs 1.10 required, friction-applied confidence bound below zero — are all restatements of one sentence: the edge is smaller than the cost of expressing it.

    The bottleneck has moved. Campaigns 1–2 failed for lack of signal. Campaign 3 failed for cost of execution. Those demand different next steps — and that is progress, not a synonym for “failed again.”

    3. The symbol split is a warning label

    SPY made +$237; QQQ lost −$1,327 (after costs). The concentration gate failed because 100% of positive P&L came from one symbol. Mid-to-mid, QQQ was still negative — so this is not purely a cost story; the signal itself expressed unevenly. Honest reading: the edge, if real, is narrower than “opening-range breakouts work.” It may be an SPY-morning phenomenon — or SPY may just have been the lucky half. 254 trades cannot separate those two stories. This is exactly what validation blocks exist for, and exactly why the gates refused to open them.

    4. What the 56-event table teaches

    Look at the screen table with the axis filter. Patterns worth knowing: cross-axis events beat single-axis events — the winner combined price action (breakout) with microstructure (tight spreads). Pure premium-dynamics events (the descendants of Campaign 1’s approach) cluster near zero or negative — a year of data confirms the original autopsy. The rare-event carryovers from Campaign 2 (U04, U05, M02, M03, M11) finally got real sample sizes and mostly deflated — their small-sample sparkle was noise, which is precisely what re-testing with power is for.

    5. Why “just improve execution” is half right and half dangerous

    Arithmetic: if entries could rest at the midpoint instead of crossing the spread (a limit order that gets filled), the toll drops by roughly half the spread each way — about $4–5 on these contracts. +$3.34 − $1.30 commissions + spread savings ≈ breakeven-to-positive. Tempting. Two honest cautions. First, resting orders don’t always fill — and the fills you miss are disproportionately the ones that would have won (the market runs away from you; that is adverse selection). Second, an edge whose entire profitability depends on execution modeling is fragile evidence until proven on real fills. This is why the next campaign (already planned) starts with a feasibility atlas — an “oracle test” that computes what a perfect forecaster could earn in each corner of the option grid after realistic costs. Regions where perfection loses are closed permanently; execution research is then aimed only where the ceiling is high enough to matter.

    6. What would have to be true for this to become tradable

    In order: (a) the oracle atlas shows the L10 region has a positive ceiling at realistic costs; (b) the P&L decomposition (currently running) shows the +$3.34 comes from direction — Delta — rather than luck-shaped residue; (c) a matched-random baseline shows L10 beats random entries under identical conditions; (d) an execution study on tick data shows midpoint-ish fills are achievable often enough; (e) THEN the frozen family faces the sealed validation block, once. Five hurdles, each cheap to check, each capable of killing the idea. That’s the design: ideas die cheaply so capital doesn’t.

    7. What I would not conclude

    Not “the system almost works” — a 0.90 profit factor loses money at any scale. Not “switch to SPY only” — that decision made after seeing the split would be textbook selection bias; the split is a hypothesis for the next preregistration, not a patch for this one. Not “loosen the gates” — the gates that said no are the only reason the three campaigns’ verdicts mean anything at all. The sealed blocks (B2: 2026-05-26→06-26, C2: 06-29→07-24, C3: accruing daily) remain unspent; they are the most valuable thing this program owns, and they are only valuable because no result has ever been allowed to touch them.

    Bottom line: no deployment, correctly. One suggestive, cost-bound, symbol-concentrated edge; a 56-hypothesis map of a year of market data; and an intact confirmation ladder. The next dollar of effort goes to feasibility and execution physics — not to a 57th indicator.
